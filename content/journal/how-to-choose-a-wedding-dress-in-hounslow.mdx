Most brides come to us having already looked everywhere else first. They have scrolled through thousands of images, visited one or two chain boutiques, and come away with a folder of screenshots that somehow do not add up to a clear picture of what they actually want. That is not a failure on their part — it is a normal response to an enormous choice with an enormous price tag attached.

This guide is for anyone at that stage. It is not about persuading you to choose one kind of dress over another. It is about giving you the practical information that tends to be missing from the bridal press: what things actually cost, how long they actually take, and what the difference is between buying a dress, altering a dress, and having a dress made.

## What your budget really covers

Wedding dress pricing confuses almost everyone who hasn't done it before, because the same nominal budget can mean very different things depending on how you spend it.

A budget of £500 to £800 in the current London market gives you a choice of three broad paths. You could buy a mid-range ready-to-wear dress from a high-street bridal brand — most come with minimal alteration included, and anything more significant is quoted separately. You could buy a secondhand or sample dress from a boutique sale, which often means significant alteration work to get it fitting correctly. Or you could come to an independent studio like ours with that budget and have a dress made, particularly if your silhouette is clear and the design is not excessively complex.

None of these is the wrong choice. But they each have different lead times, different fitting processes, and different outcomes — and a bride who understands those differences will make a far better decision than one who is comparing price tags in isolation.

One thing worth saying plainly: the alteration budget is often forgotten until the dress has already arrived. Alterations for a wedding dress in London typically range from £150 to £500 depending on the scope of work. Hemming a simple gown is at the lower end. Restructuring a bodice, taking in a dress by several sizes, or extending a zip — these are at the higher end. If your budget does not include space for alterations, factor them in before you choose where to buy.

## Ready-to-wear, made-to-measure, or bespoke — what is the actual difference

These three terms are used loosely in the bridal industry, sometimes interchangeably, which makes comparison difficult. Here is what they mean in practice.

**Ready-to-wear** means the dress exists, in a specific size, and you are buying it. Alterations bring it to fit your body. Most high-street bridal and online bridal brands work this way.

**Made-to-measure** means the dress is constructed to your measurements from the outset, usually based on an existing pattern or block. The fit is considerably better from the first fitting, because the proportions are correct for your body rather than an average body. Fewer alterations are needed, though fittings are still required.

**Bespoke** means the dress is designed from scratch — from initial sketch through toile (a test garment in cheap fabric) to final construction. Bespoke work takes longer, costs more, and produces something that exists nowhere else. For a bride with a specific vision that does not correspond to anything available commercially, it is often the most efficient route — though not the cheapest one.

## Lead times: what is realistic

The lead time question is one of the most common sources of stress in the bridal fitting process, usually because brides underestimate how much time the work takes.

A full bespoke commission — design, toile, construction, multiple fittings — requires a minimum of four to six months. Six to nine months is more comfortable and gives you flexibility if life gets in the way. Twelve months or more is ideal for a complex design or heavily embellished piece.

Alterations work has shorter timelines, but "short" is relative. For standard alterations — hemming, taking in, small structural changes — six to eight weeks is reasonable. For more involved work, including restructuring the bodice, replacing a closure, or adding embellishment, twelve weeks is safer.

Last-minute alteration work can sometimes be accommodated, but it depends entirely on what is in the diary and what the work involves. We have completed alterations in eight days. We have also turned away work that genuinely could not be done to an acceptable standard in the time available. Honesty about timelines is more useful to a bride than false reassurance.

## What to bring to your first fitting

A first fitting goes much better when you arrive prepared. Here is what makes a practical difference.

**The undergarments you plan to wear on the day.** The fit of a dress changes significantly depending on whether you are wearing a strapless bra, a corset, or nothing at all. Your fitter cannot pin correctly to a foundation that won't be there on the day.

**Your shoes, or shoes of the same heel height.** Hem length is set in relation to the floor when you are wearing your wedding shoes. A heel of two inches changes everything.

**A clear sense of what you do not want.** If you have five images of dresses you love, that is useful but not decisive — most brides who love those images could love several different dresses. Far more useful is knowing that you do not want a neckline lower than the collarbone, or that you need full mobility in your arms for a particular ceremony. Constraints clarify.

**Patience.** A first fitting for a bespoke commission or a significant alteration typically lasts between 60 and 90 minutes. If you are coming in with a ready-to-wear dress for assessment, it may be shorter. Either way, a first fitting is a conversation, not a transaction.

## Why Hounslow matters for South Asian bridal wear

Hounslow has a significant South Asian community, and the bridal needs of that community are specific in ways that are not always understood by studios outside it. A lehenga has different construction from a Western gown. An anarkali has different proportions. A saree blouse has intricate embellishment that must not be disturbed in alteration. A sharara moves differently from a skirt.
