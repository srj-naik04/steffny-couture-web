Your dress has arrived. Perhaps it came from a boutique, perhaps from an online retailer, perhaps it has been sitting in a box since your mother wore it. Whatever the story, it needs to fit you — specifically you, at your current measurements, on a particular day — and you need someone to make that happen without damaging something that matters.

This guide walks through the alteration process as we run it at the Hounslow studio: what happens at each stage, what you should expect from your fitter, and the timeline questions that come up most often.

## When to start: the short answer

Six to eight weeks before the wedding is the minimum for most standard bridal alterations. Twelve weeks is considerably more comfortable. If your wedding is fewer than six weeks away and you have not yet spoken to a fitter, contact the studio now — not to cause alarm, but because time genuinely affects what is possible.

The reason is not that the physical work takes that long — a hem can be completed in a day. It is that a well-fitted dress requires a process: a first assessment fitting, followed by construction work, followed by a second fitting to check the result, followed by any refinements, followed by a final fitting before collection. Each fitting needs to be spaced by at least one to two weeks to allow the work in between to be completed properly. Compressing that timeline means compressing the quality of the result.

For more complex alterations — restructuring a bodice, resizing by more than two dress sizes, replacing a closure system, or adding structural elements — start twelve weeks out as a firm minimum, sixteen as a preference.

## What happens at the first fitting

The first fitting is an assessment, not a commitment. Bring the dress, bring your wedding shoes (or shoes of equivalent heel height), and bring your wedding undergarments. All three affect the fit in ways that cannot be estimated without them.

Your fitter will put the dress on you and work through the garment systematically — bodice, waist, hips, hem, closure, any structural elements. They will pin adjustments, take notes, and give you a clear picture of what is involved. A reputable fitter gives you a written quote before any work begins. That quote should list every alteration item and its cost.

A first fitting for a wedding dress typically takes between 45 minutes and an hour and a half, depending on how much work is anticipated. If a fitter assesses and quotes you in ten minutes, that is worth noting.

At Steffny Couture, the first consultation is free. The quote produced at that consultation is firm — we do not add costs later unless you request additional work.

## How many fittings do you need

The number of fittings depends entirely on the scope of the work. For minimal alterations — say, a straightforward hem and a small bodice adjustment — two fittings may be sufficient: one to assess and pin, one to try the finished work. For more significant alterations, three is standard: assessment, work fitting (checking the construction before final finishing), and final fitting for collection.

A bride who is having a dress taken in significantly, or who has a complex closure or train, should expect three fittings as a minimum. If fittings are being rushed into fewer sessions than the work warrants, that is a sign that corners are being cut.

Between fittings, your fitter should be working. If there is no clear account of what is being done between your first and second fitting, ask.

## What alterations are and are not possible

Brides sometimes arrive with expectations that outpace what the dress can physically accommodate. A frank assessment is more useful than false optimism, so here is a realistic picture.

**What is generally straightforward:**
- Hemming (even trains and complex multi-layer skirts, though these take longer)
- Taking in the bodice or waist by one or two sizes
- Adjusting a zip or clasp closure
- Shortening or adding to straps
- Removing or adding a bustle

**What requires more significant work:**
- Taking in by more than two dress sizes — the dress may need to be substantially reconstructed, and in some cases the original shape of the garment changes
- Adding structural support (boning, internal cups, corset back) where none existed
- Significant neckline alterations — these affect the entire structure of the bodice
- Adding or extending a train on a dress not designed for one

**What may not be possible:**
- Letting out a dress that has no seam allowance — if the construction does not leave room, there is no room
- Altering heavily embellished garments without disturbing the embellishment — each piece must be assessed individually
- Adding a feature that requires fabric not present in the dress — unless you have matching fabric to source or the addition can be made in a complementary material

An honest fitter will tell you if what you want cannot be done, or if it can technically be done but the result will not be what you are picturing. That conversation is better had at the first fitting than after the work is started.

## The final fitting and collection

The final fitting is the last opportunity to check everything. Try the dress on in full — shoes, undergarments, and ideally the hair trial completed so you have a sense of how the neckline and back sit. Walk, sit, stand with your arms at your sides and raised. Dance if you need to — it is not a silly test. A dress that binds when you lift your arms at the reception is not a well-fitted dress.

If anything is not right at the final fitting, say so. A good fitter will want to know. If you leave with something that does not feel correct, the time to fix it is before collection, not two days before the wedding.

## Looking after the dress once it is finished

Once the alterations are complete and the dress is home:

**Hanging.** If the dress has a structured bodice or a train, hang it on a padded hanger in a breathable garment bag. Do not keep it in a plastic cover — any moisture trapped inside will affect the fabric over time.

**Steaming.** Creases from transit or storage can usually be steamed out the day before the wedding. Your fitter or dry cleaner can do this, or a handheld steamer on a low setting works on most fabrics. Avoid direct iron contact on lace, tulle, or heavily embellished areas.

**Transport.** Carry the dress flat if at all possible, particularly if it has a long skirt or train. A dress folded and crammed into a car boot will need re-steaming.

**Day-of prep.** Allow yourself thirty minutes more than you think you need to put the dress on correctly, particularly if it has a corset back or hook-and-eye closure. Someone familiar with the closure is helpful — the best person for that is not the most willing, it is the most patient.

## A note on timing

Wedding planning involves many moving parts, and the dress is often managed around everything else rather than as a priority. The practical advice is simple: book the alteration consultation as soon as the dress arrives — ideally the week it arrives. Waiting does not improve the options and may reduce them.

Bring the dress, bring your shoes, and come with an open mind about what the process involves. Everything else can be worked out in the room.
