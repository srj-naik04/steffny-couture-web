South Asian weddings in West London are not a monolith. A Hindu ceremony in Hayes has different visual traditions from a Sikh wedding in Slough. A Gujarati bride's expectations of her lehenga differ from those of a Punjabi bride for her anarkali. And within each community, personal style, family expectation, and the venue itself all shape what the bridal party actually wears.

At Steffny Couture, we have worked with South Asian brides in Hounslow and the surrounding area for over twenty years. This guide is not a checklist of rules — it is a practical overview of the silhouettes, the fitting considerations, and the coordination questions that come up most often when we are working with a South Asian bridal party.

## Understanding the silhouettes

**Lehenga choli** is the most common South Asian bridal silhouette in West London at the moment. The three-piece set — blouse (choli), skirt (lehenga), and dupatta — gives a bride and her fitter a great deal of structural flexibility. The choli can be tailored closely to the torso, the lehenga skirt can be adjusted for length and fullness, and the dupatta can be pinned or draped according to ceremony type.

A lehenga that has been properly fitted moves very differently from one that has not. The waistband should sit at the natural waist with no gap at the back and no pulling at the front. The choli closure — whether hook-and-eye, cord, or zip — should close without strain. The hem of the skirt should just graze the floor, not trail so heavily that the bride cannot move. These are not cosmetic adjustments — they affect comfort across a wedding day that typically lasts twelve or more hours.

**Anarkali** suits have a different structural logic from a lehenga. The long flared kurta over churidar trousers is a column silhouette that reads elegantly in photographs but requires specific attention to proportion — particularly the length of the kurta relative to the wearer's height and the angle of the flare. An anarkali that is too long looks shapeless. One that is too short has a different problem. Hemming an anarkali correctly, particularly in embellished fabric, is detailed work.

**Sharara and gharara** are both trouser-and-top combinations, but they differ in cut. A sharara has wide-leg trousers that flare from the hip, while a gharara flares from the knee. Both are less common than the lehenga in the current West London bridal market but are periodically in demand, particularly for sangeet and mehendi events. The construction of the trouser flare is more complex than it appears, and the hem — which typically brushes the floor — needs to be set precisely.

**Fusion pieces** are an increasingly common request. A bride who wants a choli bodice with South Asian embroidery over a Western ball skirt, or a structured Western-style corset with an embellished lehenga skirt, is asking for something that requires a fitter who works fluently in both traditions. Those pieces do not fit neatly into either a bridal boutique or a traditional South Asian fashion house — they are best handled by a couturier who has made both.

## Colour: what works for bridal parties

South Asian weddings tend to have more intentional colour planning than their Western counterparts. The bride's colours are often decided first, and the bridal party — bridesmaids, the groom's family, key family members — colour-coordinate around them.

A few practical points from the studio:

**Matching fabric is not the same as matching colour.** Two metres of the same fabric, dyed in the same batch, will look identical. Six lehengas in "the same red" sourced from different suppliers will not. If you are dressing a bridal party in coordinating colours, sourcing fabric from a single supplier and having the garments made in the same studio is the most reliable way to achieve consistency.

**Contrast can be more effective than match.** A bride in deep maroon with bridesmaids in blush or antique gold creates a more layered visual than a bride in maroon with bridesmaids in a lighter maroon. This is not a rule, but it is worth considering.

**Mixed bridal parties — South Asian bridesmaids in Indian wear, Western bridesmaids in gowns — can work very well,** provided there is a unifying element: a shared colour palette, matching dupatta fabric as a sash or shawl, or consistent jewellery. What makes a mixed party feel cohesive is intention, not uniformity.

## The practical side of dressing a bridal party

Dressing four, six, or eight people for a wedding is a project management task as much as it is a creative one. From the studio's point of view, the challenges are predictable:

**Scheduling.** Everyone has different availability. We handle this by allowing bridesmaids to come in for individual fittings within a window, rather than requiring the whole party to be present simultaneously. The bride sets the direction at the first session; the bridesmaids are fitted against that template.

**Bodies change between commission and wedding day.** A bridesmaid who is fitted six months before the wedding may be in a different place — physically — on the day. We build enough time into the schedule for a final fitting in the last three to four weeks before the wedding, when further changes are unlikely.

**Pregnancy.** More common than brides expect, and less of a problem than it seems. A garment that is fitted on a pregnant bridesmaid needs to be either designed to accommodate growth, or held back until the third trimester when the final measurements are more stable. Both approaches work; the key is acknowledging the situation early rather than hoping it works itself out.

**One person who cannot make the fittings.** This happens regularly. We take detailed measurements and work from those, with the understanding that a last-minute fitting on or near the wedding day will probably be necessary. It is not ideal, but it is manageable.

## Choosing between custom and ready-to-wear for your bridal party

Ready-to-wear South Asian occasion wear is widely available in Hounslow and Southall, and for bridesmaids who want a degree of autonomy in their choice, it can work well. The trade-off is in consistency — sizes vary across brands, embellishment is fixed, and alteration is always required.

Custom-made bridesmaid pieces for a South Asian wedding take longer and cost more per garment, but they produce a consistent result across a group of people with different body types. For a wedding where the visual impression of the bridal party matters — and most brides have a view on this — the investment in custom or made-to-measure pieces is usually worth it.

A middle path that works well: brides source coordinating fabric (often from Southall market or specialist wholesale suppliers) and bring it to the studio. We make the pieces from a shared pattern adjusted to each person's measurements. The result is consistent in colour and finish without the full cost of a bespoke design process.

## What to bring to the first consultation

If you are coming in for a South Asian bridal commission or alteration, there are a few things that make the first appointment more productive.

**The garment, if you already have it.** For alterations, this is essential. For a commission, bring any reference images you have — from wedding photography, Instagram, or designer lookbooks. Screenshots on your phone are fine.

**Your ceremony and reception plan.** A bride who is changing outfits during the day — common in South Asian weddings — needs each garment thought through independently. The ceremony lehenga may need to allow for a specific ritual; the reception outfit may need to accommodate hours of dancing. These are different briefs.

**A clear sense of embellishment.** Heavy zardosi or mirror-work requires more construction time and more fitting time than a relatively plain garment. Being realistic about the amount of hand-embellishment involved helps set an accurate expectation of cost and timeline.

**Your family's expectations, if relevant.** South Asian bridal wear often has a family dimension that Western gowns do not — a colour may carry meaning, a particular fabric may have been sourced on a trip to India or Pakistan, or there may be expectations around modesty or coverage. Steffi has navigated all of these conversations before. Bringing them to the first appointment rather than raising them later is always the better approach.
